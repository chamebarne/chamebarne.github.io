Talking about feminism and video games on the internet is a bit of a death wish. Even the most basic feminist critique—that maybe a lot of female characters are designed, all other character traits notwithstanding, with men in mind—is often met with condescending dismissal or even open misogynistic vitriol by the people being critiqued in a *bodily* rejection of a truth which would implicate their thoughts or behaviors as even slightly self-serving. The critique is usually not even that women cannot or should not be conventionally attractive, nor that men cannot or should not create female characters, only that women are commonly portrayed in ways that seem more to satisfy men than women by design. Why should men get the final say for so many characters who do not represent them? At its root, that is the basic complaint. And even that is too critical for many men who wish to ogle in peace. One begins to realize how gamergate was able to happen: despite women playing video games since their creation, video games as a hobby and an industry still house some very loud misogynists (many of whom will vehemently deny hating women while at once spewing lines like, “You should have stayed out of our video games.”). Online spaces being so inundated with people who hate women makes trying to talk about feminist issues in video games… arduous at times. Sisyphean, even.

But I can’t help myself. I have loved video games since I was a child, and being a woman who loves video games makes it hard to sit quietly by as I see people with their whole chests disparage women I love for reasons they do not hold against men. I am going to talk about feminism in games until I am dead. Now, one of the things that makes misogyny so hard to talk about in general is men’s propensity to not realize they are participating in it. Which only makes sense—it is hard to notice harms and double standards which do not directly affect you, and even if you do notice them, there is the additional challenge of taking them seriously when they may seem small or insignificant. I could go on and on about how living in a society that does not listen to you undermines its claims of equality, and how much experiencing it fucking sucks. But I do not want to simply rant. Given some of the unfortunate interactions I have had the displeasure of having on twitter recently with disgruntled men, I have decided it might be worth it to more broadly wax feminist about this topic that is dear to me in a thoughtfully articulated way. Cathartic as it can be to insult men online, where I can really express my thoughts and feelings with flourish is through writing and art. I do not write this post to be taken seriously by detractors, but to share myself more gracefully to the people who already trust me where it matters. I want to talk about misogyny in and around video games in a way that helps articulate the justified feelings of frustration women have when trying to discuss the things they care about. I will not be saying anything novel or revelatory within the history of feminism; I will only be sharing some ideas that I think are salient in our current historical moment. I am going to talk about the movement of feminism in opposition to the patriarchy and its particular systemic and interpersonal expressions, specifically within the context of video games—or, more accurately, the people who play them. Perhaps predictably, I am going to make some of my points by talking about *Cyberpunk*. Again, I can’t help myself. But I will also talk about gaming communities and gender politics. And, while this post will not explicitly feature any citations so as to be more casual and accessible in presentation, feminist theory serves as the backdrop for my arguments.

Feminism in video games of course goes beyond concerns about the male gaze and its inclination towards sexy, scantily-clad women. We may rightly refer to the male gaze as “objectifying” for this tendency, but _to be an object in the eye of another person_ can manifest in myriad nefarious ways, not solely sexual. So, I want to start by talking about how men talk about women. My examples will be about video game women, but you may notice that these observations often apply to the way men talk about real women as well—which is no small part of why talking about feminism in games (and all art) is so important, but I digress. The first thing to notice is that men do not talk about female characters the way they talk about male characters. The discrepancy is invisible to those who perpetuate it, and insultingly apparent to those who have noticed. Recently I made twitter posts defending the characters Evelyn Parker and Claire in _Cyberpunk 2077_, and they both drew out a lot of people eager to share their thoughts. Some of them are not even worth focusing on here; the people being explicitly transphobic and inflammatory are obviously not even pretending to care about women, and while that is a problem on its own, it is pretty easy for most people to agree that such people are maladjusted bigots. For our purposes, I am more concerned with a startling number of the other replies I received, in which, for example, Claire was properly gendered with hatred of her character justified in the same breath. Some of these replies allowed their misogyny to peek through the cracks more obviously than others, calling Claire choice words such as “annoying,” “insufferable,” and “bitch.” These comments have something in common with the next group, namely couching their disproportionate taste for a female character in a litany of reasons justifying why the character is just so awful and duplicitous and worthy of hatred. There is something uniquely insidious about this kind of comment, I think. Specifically, these commenters seem to fully believe that their rationalizations are completely detached from misogyny, as though they are perfectly neutral. Conversations around Evelyn Parker follow the same kind of trend: these people would have you believe they dislike her purely on the basis of noble, moral grounds—it has nothing to do with her being a woman! Even when trying to defend her character, men feel the need to qualify that they still think she was foolish or backstabbing, as though barring her suffering they would have little to positively say about her, or no other reason to defend her. None of the men eager to criticize her on these grounds realize their comments about Evelyn are calloused and unfair because they believe they are neutrally describing a situation in which someone got in over her head and simply suffered the inevitable consequences of that. Mind you, these “consequences” consist of her brain being fried by hackers and being raped repeatedly before being sent to a sexually exploitative ripperdoc and then finally trafficked to the Scavengers for them to exploit her further. There is no framing of this rationale that does not come across as victim-blaming—that somehow she should have expected to be treated that way, and was “dumb” or an “idiot” for putting herself in that position. And yet, when I saw a post correctly pointing out the misogyny of beloved rockerboy Johnny Silverhand, whom fans idolize and make sympathetic, which effectively softens the overall perception of his problematic and destructive behavior, men were rejecting the idea that he might treat women unfairly at all. The very idea that he _could_ treat a woman with an unconscious bias was denied as a frivolous suggestion, despite it clearly happening on screen numerous times throughout the game, on top of all of his other shitty actions that you learn about and see first-hand. If you try to give characters like Claire and Evelyn the same sympathy and grace and benefit of the doubt as Johnny, suddenly the misogynist becomes a moral authority who feels compelled to share his valuable insight and pass judgment on the matter.

The double standard I am really alluding to is the tendency for people to engage in disproportionate and undue moral scrutiny of female characters versus male characters. My specific complaint here is not so much that the rationale is incorrect (though it often is), so much as that it is unequally applied. Greater expectations are placed on women to behave a certain way (usually a male-pleasing one) than on men, and _these expectations are not explicit_. I consider this kind of misogyny to be uniquely insidious because not only is it veiled and disguised, it is largely unconscious. It is, in a word, systemic. It is constituted by speech, behavior, and ideas which are perpetuated and reinforced through mundane interactions and decisions. It is thoughtless. The fundamental problem at its root is that people objectify women—literally treat them as objects compared to men. Men are treated by society as fully-autonomous, sympathetic, often misunderstood creatures by default; in a word, as people. The reason feminism exists at all is because the same treatment has not been given by default to women in most times and places except by women fighting to secure and preserve some semblance of equality. And, if I can be perfectly honest and a little blunt, it is foolish to think that this inequality of treatment fully disappears after women’s suffrage and property ownership are written into law. My mother has reminded me on occasion how women could not even get a credit card in the US until the 1970s—a time that is in living memory for a large part of the population, including my mother! Sexist attitudes do not change so quickly just because laws have been put into place (and in fact, sometimes laws are put into place because of people’s refusal to otherwise act without bias). Some attitudes are as generational as wealth. My point is that a person can be misogynistic without realizing it, and it results in the inequality and dehumanization of women. I do not think this claim is even remotely controversial.

If, for some reason, you doubt these claims I am making, let’s do a small exercise. Think of a female character you hate (maybe Skylar from Breaking Bad? She’s a popular choice). Physically count on your fingers the number of men you hate in the same franchise. How many are there? Are your reasons for hating them as numerous and thoroughly articulated in your mind as those justifying your hatred of the woman? Is this the _first time_ you have devoted this much thought to why you hate those male characters? Are there, conveniently, no men in the franchise you find detestable? Depending on how you answer these questions, you might, in fact, be a little more misogynistic than you realize. To be clear, generally, the feminist critique is not that your bias damns you to hell or makes you an abuser or something. We all have biases we are not aware of. The goal is to bring them to the forefront and try to keep them in check; the goal is to learn to shake off as much bias as possible, so that treating others equally comes naturally. No one is perfect at it, which is exactly why critique is necessary in the first place.

But becoming aware of one’s own biases, let alone shaking them off, gets touchy. No one _likes_ to be told that they may actually hold views that are harmful or unfair towards another group of people. It takes practice to be able to say, “I might be wrong. I might be wrong in ways I do not realize. I might even be wrong in ways I have a hard time accepting, precisely because I am so certain I am right.” The moral tension which arises when confronted with one’s own biases, in this case the bias of misogyny, leads us to my second talking point, which is about the communities which form around video games and how their dispositions towards and inclusion of women influence their general acceptance or dismissal of certain social mores and moral causes. A stark contrast on these very points exists, for example, between the so-called “fighting game community” (FGC) and the predominant playerbase of _The Sims_. I am a big fighting game fan. I have invested a few hundred hours across franchises and at least sampled just about everything the genre has to offer. But I have no trouble describing myself as a “casual” player (a loaded term, but again, I digress). Still, I keep up with the general news around fighting games, which means, on some level, observing and occasionally engaging with the people more heavily involved in it. One thing I have noticed over the past five years of playing is that the FGC is, by outward appearances, mostly men. Surely there are women who play—myself included—but they tend to stay quiet, and it is not hard to figure out why. I have been on the periphery of the FGC for years, and it is overwhelmingly apparent that it is one of the most miserable, mean-spirited, condescending, stalwartly gatekept groups of people I have ever encountered online, and I do not think it is a stretch to say that that is because it is comprised of so many men. Many of them believe themselves to be welcoming—but their true colors quickly show themselves when you tell them that you, say, do not want to learn numpad notation, or think that fighting games are, in fact, maybe harder to learn both conceptually and mechanically than most other genres of games. No room is permitted to even have a conversation about such things, because the very suggestion of them is met by a community so insular that they have forgotten what it is like to play fighting games for the first time and to view their community from the outside—or never bothered to meaningfully think about how these things affect anyone else. It is perhaps low-hanging fruit to say this mentality is a form of toxic masculinity, but I do not know what else to call a mindset that believes one should simply get over their hangups and reservations about how they are allowed to engage in a gaming _community_. If the FGC would like to be a welcoming community in both perception and reality, it needs to listen more to the people who feel unwelcomed by it.

Talking about the FGC’s inclination towards masculine insularity serves only as preamble for the more precise point I want to draw attention to. We are already aware of the challenges women face by being visible in male-dominated spaces, such as harassment and exclusion. It should be extremely apparent that the FGC perpetuates these challenges. But this kind of community environment carries further implications and consequences, which we can see based on the discrepancies between the FGC and a community comprised mostly of women by contrast. I have never played _The Sims_, but several of my friends are long-time fans. Based on what they tell me, the playerbase skews female. Additionally, the most popular content creators for _The Sims_ are women. The broad community of people who play and/or enjoy the games and their surrounding content has uncontroversial female representation, while the FGC does not. (In fact, some of the most visible women in the FGC are trans women—and the harassment they face is unlike anything the prominent male figures experience. Their very presence is met with regular, consistent ire, and acceptance is very much an uphill battle fought for and defended by the best of its members.) We can see a big difference in the generally-accepted and promoted social and moral attitudes between these two communities by highlighting a thing they share: the meddling of the Saudi Arabian government. The fighting game franchises _Fatal Fury_ and _King of Fighters_, among others, are developed by Japanese company SNK—which was saved from total bankruptcy and dissolution by Saudi Arabia, because the prince is a big fan of their games. For some fighting game fans, this connection has turned into a cause for total boycott, myself included. The Saudi government has further tried to entrench itself into fighting games as a form of sportswashing to hide their human rights abuses. Some fans have expressed their discomfort about this connection. Would you care to guess the ways in which many people in the FGC choose to respond? Would it surprise you if I told you the response was defensiveness, whataboutism, a sudden concern for “moral hypocrisy”, and the implication that trying to do something good is, in fact, somehow _bad_ as a consequence of that hypocrisy? I could get into the weeds about the problems with these reactionary talking points (such as the inability to distinguish between buying a game from a private company in the US existing under a fascist regime versus buying a game from a company _directly owned_ by a fascist regime, or how the knee-jerk reaction from these people serves to assuage their own consciences because they do not want to feel like their consumerism may implicate them even slightly in moral wrongdoing (despite apparently not caring about moral issues to begin with?)), but it would quickly become self-indulgent. Suffice it to say that many fighting game fans want to be able to support their beloved corporations without feeling bad about it. And again we see the same problem emerge as before: people rightly raise concerns that as Saudi Arabia becomes more involved in fighting game development and events, the FGC will become even _less_ welcoming to people—disproportionately women and LGBTQ+ people. I have yet to see any big name in fighting games champion disavowing these changes as a moral cause, even when they quietly disapprove of them. The closest I have seen of a prominent personality in the FGC to a concerted effort to raise these problems _as problems_ is Sajam, and that was only in brief interactions on twitter. The biggest players and content creators will occasionally allude to these problems and imply a soft disapproval, but I have not seen any of them use their platforms and fame to encourage others to take a moral stance against Saudi Arabia’s sportswashing. I do not say this to vilify these people (well, most of them, anyway) or to diminish the good work they _do_ perform for the underprivileged people in the FGC (especially the work I am sure that I do not see, me being mostly on the periphery), so much as to explain that _what they are allowed to uncontroversially say is severely limited by the social situation of the FGC—a community that is mostly men._

_The Sims_ is published by EA, a company which was bought out by Saudi Arabia this year. By contrast to the FGC, *the most popular* Sims _content creator, lilsimsie, left the EA creator network because of Saudi Arabia’s purchase._ She no longer purchases _Sims_ add-ons at all and only uses freely accessible mods and content. She has been outspoken about the issue and done streaming events to help raise awareness. As I understand it, the overwhelming majority of the community supports her and, at the very least, shares her sentiment if they themselves have not started to financially boycott EA just yet. Hearing my best friend describe this situation was illuminating to me, especially after I had been called a hypocrite for saying that the Games Done Quick speedrunning charity event was right to drop SNK as a sponsor this past summer. We immediately identified the gender divide between the two communities. The difference in social and moral dispositions between a group in which patriarchy is deeply ingrained and one in which it is far less so is abundantly clear, almost insultingly so. Where in the former the very suggestion that monetarily supporting a certain company might have negative moral implications is widely dismissed, the latter fully accepts and openly endorses its moral causes without having to question whether they should exist in the first place.

So what, exactly, is going on with men that makes them so touchy and so reactionary about video games? What makes them so possessive about this hobby and medium specifically? Plenty of good speculation about this has been posited throughout the years, such as the male fantasy and reactionary politics, but I would like to hone in on another angle of explanation. Namely: games are serious business. I basically think that video games as a medium, and as a vehicle for misogyny, function similarly to physical sports. Many of the same observations seen in the latter can be seen in the former. We see how competitive games are often hostile to female players; how men hate losing to women; and so-called “girly” games are disparaged as inherently inferior, or not “real” games for “real” gamers. The institution of professional sports has already actualized its sexist foregone conclusion that men are superior such that women must play in their own special leagues with different rules—rather than, you know, adjusting the rules of the sports themselves such that the competition is not designed with exclusion in mind. We have to be sexist because sports are just so serious.

_Obviously_ there are, for example, players in the WNBA who out-skill plenty of players in the NBA, but we have relegated them to a smaller ball and a closer three point line, and give them less attention because they do not dunk as often. But this fact is obscured more easily in sports than in video games due to the difference in athleticism. The specific “threat” which men feel in regards to video games is that the presumed separation between men and women—the farce of inherently disproportionate power between the sexes that is built in to sports—has already been rejected by the very medium of video games. No one can seriously appeal to “biological advantages” when it comes to video games (though some men certainly try), and the whole defense of gender segregation falls apart. A medium of which men wished to claim and maintain sole ownership, it turns out, has always been enjoyed by women as well, despite the industry’s initial (and in some cases ongoing) efforts to insist it is a bloke’s world. It is harder to make a sexist point about video games sound reasonable by comparison to sports because the medium itself is physically equalized for all able-bodied players. Anyone who can use a computer or a controller can learn to play video games, and to play them well, and to play them well enough to show the irrelevance of the gender of the players. And so men fiercely flail in a desperate attempt to cling to, and reassert to themselves, their preconceived superiority in a medium they believe historically belonged solely to them. Their position is doomed to fail, and instinctively they recognize as much on a passive level; as such, many of them would rather die (or kill) than lose that sense of power. Any feminist critique of video games, any equalization of female characters or women-led causes, are perceived as active threats towards men because they expose what men cannot accept: that the medium of video games rejects their sexism in its premise.

I have said time and time again that I know for a fact that good men exist—I know many of them personally. But the things that good men all share in common which set them apart from their less exceptional brethren are their willingness to listen to women and their openness to critique. They are receptive to women’s complaints and remorseful when they mess up. I am confident that the men I know who read this post are already sympathetic enough take its contents seriously. And this post is mostly for them. It feels futile for me to try to argue with people who are predisposed not listen to me no matter how well I articulate myself simply because I am a woman (vehemently as they may deny that is the reason). Some men need to hear these perspectives from other men for there to be any chance of receptivity. I trust the good men in my life to assist my efforts to bring feminism into video game discourse. You have a reach I simply do not have by merit of your male privilege. I thank you deeply for leveraging it on women’s behalf.

And with that, I will return to being a bitch online about it.

Jamie Barne
